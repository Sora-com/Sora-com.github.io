The title of this note is quite generic, but AI is indeed such a vast topic that it would be impossible for me to find a more specific one. To make my thoughts clearer, I will do my best to divide the discussion into different points of view. 

# Intelligence as an obtainable resource and societal differences 

I will start with this point of view because it is the one I've been coming back to most recently. Right now, US companies (OpenAI, Anthropic) hold the 'key' to the tree of knowledge. Of course, since it's all proprietary and secret, we can never know whether what we see is the true quality of the models. 

Perhaps the models are better than they show, or perhaps they are worse. In any case, looking back just a year, one can clearly see that the pace of development is increasing rapidly. At a rate no one expected. 

Is it a bubble? Will it pop? Is it "real" intelligence? These are questions I will ponder later in the note. But for now, let's say it is what they sell it as: a machine for generating intelligence. With enough money, energy and time, no question will remain unanswered. What does that mean for the global order? 

In my opinion, AI should be treated as a resource measured for each country, just as we measure levels of education, energy or poverty. The fact that the biggest models are being created in secret, in the hands of trillionaire companies that are the only ones with unlimited access to them, while the rest of the world faces usage limits, *while* those companies are subject to a single country, makes this all terrifying. 

I am an EU citizen, and I believe this is a political issue. Politicians nowadays don't talk about investing in technology or AI. Has anyone thought of a "Spanish AI" or a "French AI"? And by this I don't mean a private company founded in Europe. I mean a national model funded by the state, giving the citizens of that country access to it, perhaps even for free (paid for by taxes). Technological progress is accelerating tenfold for the people who have access to these models. 

Our scientists are being forced to work with closed, proprietary models in order to keep up with the latest changes, mostly out of their own pocket (although institutions are increasingly stepping in to provide these services). But this doesn't change the fact that what we are doing is feeding these foreign companies. In fact, if we keep feeding them (which, realistically, is inevitable), we could become dependent on black-box models from other countries. For science. How does that make you feel? 

For me, what I'm thinking is that we're severely cooked. By 'we' I mean European citizens. If not per-country models, I believe we should at least push for funding European models. We cannot keep depending on foreign ones. 

One could argue that the solution is to use open-source models, but the fact is that we cannot rely on them staying open-source forever. 

# What does the future of AI look like? 

I believe that, for now, the most significant changes will come from AI harnesses rather than from improvements to the models themselves. For example, a harness and a usable API that let AI agents operate surgical equipment (something I read about online a few days ago). 

However, it is hard to say that these will be the only significant changes we will see. Looking at the timeline, the models I use most every day (Opus 5.5 and Sonnet) have improved at a crazy rate. Perhaps in the not-so-distant future we will find ourselves with models far better than the ones we have now, although that is hard to imagine. 

# The philosophical limit of transformer models 

Is AI *really* intelligent? What does intelligence mean? 

Let's try this definition: an intelligent being is capable of creating something completely new and distinct from anything that existed before. 

When we think about AI, it seems easy to see that a model based on the transformer architecture doesn't meet this definition: it cannot build something it hasn't been trained on. 

However, what about humans? This is really a question for psychologists and neuroscientists. How does learning work in the human mind? 

Think about the theses written each year by millions of university graduates and PhD students. How many of them are 'original'? What about the Nobel Prizes awarded? Are they groundbreaking? Or rather, are they a careful study of other studies? Something along the lines of "this technology, but applied to field X". 

Let's talk about the recent AI discoveries in math and science that OpenAI has announced: the [722 math manuscripts](https://github.com/openai/math) it published at the beginning of October and, just a week ago, [three new results in math, computer science and physics](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/). One could see the news and think: "We're doomed, AI is discovering things we weren't capable of." But is this true? 

AI cannot build anything out of nothing, and probably neither can we (can you imagine a color that doesn't exist, or a face you've never seen before?). 

Could these AI discoveries simply be the result of AI being nothing more than that? An AI model isn't an engineer or a specialist in any field. It is a consumer of information and a producer of answers. When dealing with scientific questions, there are millions of articles, theories and materials to work through, especially in the information age we live in. 

What I mean is that AI is not a generator of intelligence, but a really capable filter and weaver of it. It cannot generate the raw material. But that is also its strength: while we have qualities AI lacks (initiative, for example), AI has different ones: no bias toward any technical approach (or does it? We'll come back to this in the next section), no tiredness, and no limit on the information it can reach. 

# Feed it dirt and it will spit dirt 

When training any kind of machine learning model, no matter how good your algorithm is, if your training data is bad, your model is going to be bad. 

What happens when a machine learning model is trained on data it has generated itself? 

When you ask an AI agent to build a certain kind of app, 99% of the time it will recommend the same tools, architecture, stack, etc. How many AI-built Supabase + Cloudflare Workers apps exist nowadays? 

What happens when humans stop creating and AI starts feeding on its own data? In machine learning this is known as *model collapse*. (Could this eventually slow down the development of AI models?)

# Conclusion

I started this note by saying that AI is too vast a topic for a more specific title, and after writing it, I still think so. But there is one thread running through all of it: AI is only as good as what we feed it, and right now, what we feed it, and who gets to use it, is decided by a handful of companies in a single country.

If AI is a filter and weaver of human knowledge rather than a generator of it, then the real resource is not the model, but the knowledge and the people who keep producing it. That is exactly why depending on foreign black-box models worries me: we would be handing over our raw material and paying to get it back, while the incentive to keep creating our own slowly fades.

I don't think the answer is to reject AI. I use it every day. But I believe Europe needs its own models, and all of us need to keep thinking, writing and building things ourselves. Otherwise, the dirt we end up being fed might be our own.
